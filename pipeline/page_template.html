Where the two numbers come from

Every word on this page carries two numbers. They come from different sources, they are worked out in different ways, and neither one knows about the other. That is the point of the page: if the defining level were a restatement of frequency there would be nothing here to add.

How the frequency rank is worked out

It is a count of how often people say the word, and nothing else. Nobody judged any word to be easy or hard at any point.

  1. Count every word in the subtitles.
  2. Merge inflections onto the base word, so falo, falas and falou all count towards falar and the verb is ranked once rather than forty times.
  3. Merge the pronoun back where Portuguese hyphenates one onto a verb: deixa-me is not a word of its own, so its count goes to deixar. This is a quarter of the raw Portuguese list.
  4. Drop what is not vocabulary — anything under 10 occurrences, 1,737 personal names, and past rank 25,000 anything the lemma list does not recognise, which is where the subtitle tail turns into names, untranslated English and scanning debris.
  5. Sort by the count. Rank 1 is the commonest word, o; rank 35,827 is the last one left.

The CEFR band is then a threshold on that rank — A1 is 1–1,000, A2 to 3,000, B1 to 6,000, B2 to 12,000, C1 to 25,000, C2 to 50,000. The thresholds were fitted once against CEFR-J in English and are reused for every language unchanged.

How the defining level is worked out

The defining level asks a different question: how much of the dictionary leans on this word? A word sits high when the dictionary needs it in order to explain other words, and at the bottom when no definition uses it at all.

Read every Portuguese Wiktionary definition as a set of links. água appears in the definition of chuva, so there is a link água → chuva. Definitions that only name an inflection (terceira pessoa do singular…) are thrown away, because they explain nothing. That leaves 22,824 words joined by 308,680 links.

Then peel the whole thing in rounds. Round 1 removes every word used in no definition at all. Round 2 removes every word now used in fewer than 2 definitions, round 3 every word used in fewer than 3, and so on until nothing is left. Removing a word takes its links with it, so each round pushes more words under the next round's bar. A word's level is the round it falls in: out in round 1 is D7, still standing in round 7 is D1.

The rounds are the whole idea. A plain count of uses would rate a word used by a hundred dead ends as highly as one used by a hundred words the dictionary itself needs. Who uses you counts for more than how many do.

A worked example

igual (“equal”) and porco (“pig”) are each used in exactly 67 definitions. They finish three levels apart.

porco is used only by words more specific than itself — presunto, javali, toucinho, leitão, pocilga. Fifty-five of its 67 users are already gone when round 3 starts, round 3 removes ten more, and two is fewer than three, so porco falls in that same round. igual is used at every level, including by valor, diferente, semelhante and comparar — words the dictionary needs in turn, so they stay, and they hold igual up until round 6. Type either word into Find a word above.

The formal name for the rounds is the out-degree k-core decomposition, and a word's level is its core number: D1 is core 6, D7 is core 0. Words inside a definition are matched to the word list through forms.pt.json, the same form-to-base-word map the app already ships.

The distribution

Each step roughly doubles — the same shape BAND-1 already uses for CEFR, and seven tabs fit the existing band row without bucketing. Narrow the frequency window and watch the shape migrate: the head of the word list does the defining, the tail piles into D7.

Content words only hides the 218 closed-class words and 18 metalanguage terms, which is where D1's o que a de um live. It filters the display; the levels themselves are unchanged, because restricting the graph costs two levels of resolution and reorders nothing.

What to watch for

Read it as how much the dictionary leans on a word, never as easy → hard. olá and uau are A1 vocabulary at D7, because no definition is ever written in terms of "hello". That is the measure stating a fact about the dictionary, not about the word.

It is not a taxonomy either. animal sits at D2 and água at D3, though one is a category and the other a substance; planta is D3 beside olho and boca. What separates them is how often a definition reaches for the word, not how general its meaning is. The D1 list makes the same point from the other end: a quarter of it is palavra oração expressar etc pronome preposição — the vocabulary of writing definitions rather than of naming things.

The uncovered third is 13,003 words. 1,191 are inflection-only Wiktionary entries that could inherit their base word's level. The remaining 11,754 have no Wiktionary entry at all and read, in frequency order, cincar miudar john jack dessar sam farar mr michael charlie — largely names and debris that the name list and dictGate already missed, so this doubles as a quality probe on the existing word list.
